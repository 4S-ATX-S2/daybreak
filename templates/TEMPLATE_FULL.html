The Craft · A Daily Note on People & Presence

The first thirty seconds

Tonight several thousand strangers will be within a few hundred yards of this door, and a good number of them will walk past it. That makes today a useful day to say something that sounds like hospitality and is actually security: rapport is the cheapest intelligence collection there is. People tell things to staff they have a relationship with, however slight. The guest who mentions that someone has been sitting in the stairwell, or that a car has been idling at the service entrance for an hour, is almost always a guest whose name somebody used the day before. Nobody reports anything to a uniform they have never spoken to.

The craft of it is smaller than people think. Use the name once, early, and then stop — used twice it is warmth, used five times it is a script, and everyone can hear the difference. Ask something that has a real answer. "How are you?" is a formality and gets a formality back; "how was the drive in?" is a question, and a question starts an exchange. Stand beside, not in front — geometry does half the work of tone, and nobody has ever felt at ease being addressed head-on by someone in uniform. And remember one thing that isn't in the system: not the room number, which we already have, but the dog, the delayed flight, the reason they came. Recalling it tomorrow costs nothing and buys something that cannot be bought any other way.

The last part is the hardest and it is mostly restraint: let it be brief. Rapport is not conversation, and the person who wants to get to their room and is held there by a friendly member of staff has been charged for the privilege. Thirty seconds, done well, beats five minutes done thoroughly — and thirty seconds is all it takes to become a person that someone will speak to later.
