The Craft · A Daily Note on People & Presence

On a day when nothing happens, nobody is looking. Every venue is dark, the city is closed, there is no event to work around. That is not a light day — it is a day with no external structure, and structure is what has carried your attention all week. A game day tells you where to stand and when to be sharp; today you decide that yourself, about forty times, quietly, while tired. Observation is a skill, not a state of alertness. Alertness is effortful and decays inside twenty minutes; observation is a habit of comparison and lasts a full shift. Fix your baseline early — walk your area in the first hour and notice what normal looks like today, because today's normal is not Saturday's. Then you are not scanning for threats, you are noticing changes, which is cheaper and far more reliable. Look for what should be there and isn't — the propped door, the missing cone, the car in one spot for three hours, the bag with nobody near it. Absence is what the eye skips. And say what you see to one other person: half of what gets missed was seen by somebody who assumed it was already known.

Readiness · Safety, Security & CrisisTopic 6 of 12 — Medical Emergencies & The First Five Minutes
Know This

In a hotel, the first five minutes belong to whoever is standing there — not to the paramedics, and usually not to a nurse. Two situations account for most of what you will meet: someone collapses, and someone is choking. For a collapse the early question is are they breathing normally — agonal gasping is not breathing, and it is what people most often mistake for a sign of life. On a 105° day add heat: confusion, stumbling, or a guest who has stopped sweating is an emergency, not a rest-and-water situation.

Do This Today

Find your nearest AED and say the location out loud. Not “by the gym” — the actual cabinet. Know who on your shift is trained, and that the answer might be nobody — better said now than at the time. Know your radio call for a medical: the exact words, and who they reach. Then walk the route a crew would take with a stretcher: which door, which lift, who holds it. Two minutes, once.

If It Happens

Send a named person for the AED and a named person to meet the ambulance — “someone call 911” is how nobody calls 911. Give the address, the nearest door and the floor. Start compressions if they are not breathing normally; do not wait to be sure. Do not move a collapsed person somewhere less embarrassing — treat them where they are and screen with staff. Clear the corridor before the crew arrives, not while they are in it. Afterwards, whatever the outcome: the people who ran it come off the floor and get spoken to.
