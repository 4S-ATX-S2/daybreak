The Craft · A Daily Note on People & Presence

Watch the ones who are fine. This team has just worked six straight days at or above a hundred degrees, then a hundred-thousand-seat home opener, then a festival that finished at eleven. The person most likely to be in trouble this morning is not the one who says yesterday was brutal — that one has told you where they are, and telling you is most of the repair. It is the one who says they are fine, cheerfully and immediately, while volunteering for another double. There is nothing mysterious about why: sustained pressure is easier to carry than to put down, and being needed is steadying. So the tell is rarely distress — it is a brightness that doesn't fit the week: first in and last out on the day after the hard one, hasn't sat down, answers every question about themselves by talking about the operation. Cheerfulness on the seventh hard day is data, not reassurance. What to do is small. Don't challenge the “fine” — arguing only makes them defend it. Remove the need for it: send them on a break rather than offering one, put someone else on the double, hand them a job that requires sitting down. Make the rest a decision you made, not a favour they have to accept — accepting it is exactly what a person in that state won't do. And say out loud, by name, that the weekend went well. A hard stretch nobody marks teaches people the effort was invisible, and that is what wears them out — not the heat.

Readiness · Safety, Security & CrisisTopic 5 of 12 — Fire Alarms & Evacuation
Know This

In a hotel the alarm is almost never the emergency — the response is. Most activations are cooking, steam, dust or a contractor. But for the first ninety seconds the real one looks exactly the same, which is why “it's probably nothing” is the single most expensive sentence in the building. A high-rise does not empty all at once: we move the floor of alarm and the floors above and below it unless told otherwise. Lifts are never used. And guests will not leave a room because a bell is ringing — they leave when a person tells them to.

Do This Today

Find your two stairs. From wherever you work, locate the two nearest stairwells and know which is the designated evacuation stair. Check the doors are unlocked, lit and signed. Know where the fire alarm panel is and who reads it — if you don't know, that's the thing to ask today. If you work a floor, know how many rooms are on it. Two minutes, once.

If It Happens

Never say “false alarm” — you don't know yet, and being wrong once costs everything. Go to your assigned area, knock and speak, move people to the stair, count. Report your sweep by area — “fourteenth floor, east side, twelve rooms, all clear” — never a bare “all clear,” which tells the incident commander nothing about what was actually checked. First thirty seconds: panel, floor, stairs.
