The Craft · A Daily Note on People & Presence

The first thirty seconds. Tonight several thousand strangers will be within a few hundred yards of this door, which makes today a good day to say something that sounds like hospitality and is actually security: rapport is the cheapest intelligence collection there is. The guest who mentions that someone has been sitting in the stairwell, or that a car has been idling at the service entrance for an hour, is almost always a guest whose name somebody used the day before. Nobody reports anything to a uniform they have never spoken to. The craft of it is smaller than people think. Use the name once, early, then stop — twice is warmth, five times is a script, and everyone hears the difference. Ask something with a real answer: “how are you?” is a formality and gets one back; “how was the drive in?” starts an exchange. Stand beside, not in front — geometry does half the work of tone. Remember one thing that isn't in the system: the dog, the delayed flight, the reason they came. And the hardest part, which is restraint: let it be brief. The guest held at the desk by a friendly member of staff has been charged for the privilege. Thirty seconds done well beats five minutes done thoroughly — and thirty seconds is all it takes to become someone a person will speak to later.

Readiness · Safety, Security & CrisisTopic 4 of 12 — Crowds, Capacity & Getting People Out
Know This

Crowd injuries are almost never about violence. They are about density and flow — too many people in too little space, or two streams meeting. The dangerous moment is a counterflow: a crowd trying to leave meeting a crowd trying to arrive, or meeting a door that turns out to be locked. On a festival night the risk to this property is not the festival. It is our own lobby and drive at a quarter past eleven, when a few hundred people decide to move at once through a porte-cochère that is one lane wide.

Do This Today

Walk your own exit as a stranger would. From the bar, the pool deck, the ballroom: where does a crowd actually go, and what is the second way out if the first is blocked? Count the doors that get locked after nine — those are the ones that cause the counterflow. If you work outside, know which door you would bring people through. Two minutes, once, and you own it for the season.

If It Happens

Never push a crowd. Open a route and name it. “Through the doors to your left” moves people; “please move back” does not. Prop the second door before you need it, not after. Split arrivals from departures so they are not fighting for the same twenty feet. Never let a queue form across a fire exit or the drive lane. Then Security Duty Ext 8240. First thirty seconds: open a route, name it, split the flow.
